# Can we evolve beyond gender/sex classification? Rethinking school sports

In the realm of competitive sports, the traditional binary classification system based on biological sex (an athlete is a "boy" or a "girl") often fails to ensure fair and inclusive competition. 

Within single-sex categories, there can be significant disparities in height, weight, and muscle mass among athletes, which can affect fairness many sports. At a global level, extreme genetic diversity can mean particular groups are significantly more able to perform well in a give sport.

In youth sports, the age at which athletes hit puberty can significantly impact their physical capabilities, leading to imbalances in competitions that are organized strictly by age without considering physical maturity.

In recreational and school sports, athletes are often grouped by age or grade, ignoring skill level, which can lead to mismatches in abilities and discourage participation or development. 

Cut-off dates for team or coaching assignments in sports[^1] can sometimes create imbalances, particularly in youth competitions. This is due to the significant physical and skill development that can occur over a short time in children and adolescents. If a cut-off date is set for a year group you're going to have a group that includes young people with a whole year's difference in physical development.

These examples highlight that the sex-based, binary classification system may not always be the best approach to organizing sports in a way that ensures fair and inclusive competition.

Recent debates surrounding the participation of transgender athletes in women's sports have further highlighted the limitations of this system. 

I've started thinking about how other sports organisations ensure fairness like using age-spread groups in fencing, or weight classes in wrestling or boxing. I am particularly interested in the Paralympics[^2] model, to create a more equitable playing field for all athletes.

The Paralympic system of classification intends to guarantee fair competition between all athletes. Athletes go through the classification process before taking part in a Paralympic competition, during which they are assessed and allocated a sport class in accordance with the degree and nature of their eligible impairments.

Of particular interest is that this system is recognised as fair globally and the community of athletes -- buy agreeing to engage with the process -- is part of ensuring the system as a whole functions well. I don't doubt there are failures and exceptions, but this seems like a very promising model to find a new fair to improve fairness and inclusion.


## The current challenge

School sports are the breeding ground for athletic talent, shaping the future of competitive sports. Yet, the conventional classification system based on gender does not account for the wide spectrum of physical abilities and genetic diversity among athletes. 

I empathise with a young girl who has poured her entire teenage life into competitive athletics: the injuries; the sacrifices; the 5am starts; missing out on socials etc. 

But it could be that the whole frame of competitive sports is wrong.

Specifically, we assume boy/girl is a helpful way to classify sports participation to include fairness. But that's simply not true, the sheer laws of physics leads to athletes doing better or worse in certain sports. Nadia Comanenci was an extremely talented athlete who gave us the world's first perfect "10" score in gymnastics. I don't want to undermine her life's work, but she was fourteen in 1976, defeating her main rival Olga Korbut who was 21 at the time, having previously shined at 17 in the 1972 Olympics. Olga could have been forgiven for questioning how level that field was.

My observation is that the shapes of human bodies in sport change according to many factors and on several continuum. Look at East German swimmers (ok, steroids in the 1970s were a problem) or Kenyan runners. Genetics plays a role and we know genetics is far from binary, far from simple...

The one-size-fits-all approach overlooks individual capabilities, potentially stifling talent and fair competition.

My observation is that perhaps it's time to reconsider teaching sports on the basis of biological sex (or gender identity for that matter). It's possible that these aren't the fairest ways to group athletes.


## The Paralympics service

The Paralympics assessment service offers an exemplary model of athlete classification based on the degree of physical ability, ensuring that individuals compete with peers of roughly similar capabilities. This method fosters a competitive environment where success is determined by skill, strategy, and perseverance, rather than inherent physical advantages.

While this surfaced as a result of classes with extreme disparities, the same thinking could be extended to include more subtle distinctions


## So what's the "how might we" for school sports?

School sports should experiment with a classification system akin to the Paralympics, focusing on the physical abilities of each athlete rather than their gender. Such a system would involve evaluating the abilities of children in various sports, ensuring they compete in a fair and balanced manner.

There are all sorts of challenges with this, from complexity, cost, or impact on already over-stretched educators. 

Somewhere in that mix, there is a point where parents go on a journey and start to commit to the long drives to the regional competition, the motel overnights, and the demands for expensive equipment. If this new method could be applied in that community of parents and children at that point in their journey it might work?


## Implementation and challenges

Adopting this new classification system would require comprehensive testing and assessment protocols to accurately measure and categorize students' physical abilities. 

Ideally, those policies would be co-designed with athletes and their families, but the system needs to be a global standard so this may not be practical.

While this approach promises a more inclusive and equitable sports environment, it also presents potential challenges, such as logistical hurdles, the need for specialized training for coaches, and the risk of unintended consequences.
