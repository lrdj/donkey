# Which way does anger travel?

Two countries, the same fear, two completely different places to point it. That contrast has been sitting with me for months.

In France, the gilets jaunes began with the price of fuel — with the arithmetic of getting to the end of the month for people who work. And the chain of blame ran, almost mechanically, upward: to the state, to the executive, to the centre of power. They went after the sovereign.

In Britain, the same anxiety — flat wages, work that feels more precarious every year, public services buckling — gets offered a different outlet entirely. The other. The migrant. The boat crossing the Channel. In September 2025 the government announced a digital identity scheme and framed it, explicitly, as a weapon against illegal migration: small boats, illegal working, the "pull factor" of a job. The Prime Minister called it an open fight between his party and Reform. A centre-left government chose to run on its opponent's ground rather than offer a story of its own.

It would be easy to turn this into a fable about national character — the French revolt upward, the British blame sideways. But I don't think that's true, and I think it's the wrong lesson. A grievance has no built-in direction. Someone gives it one.

That's the whole idea, really. The same economic pain can travel vertically — toward the people with power, who might actually be able to fix it — or horizontally, toward a neighbour who can't. Which way it goes depends almost entirely on the stories available at the moment the anger arrives. France in 2018 still had a living vocabulary for blaming the centre: a long republican habit of holding the state responsible for the common good. The anger had somewhere upward to go, so it went there. Britain's centre-left has largely surrendered that vocabulary, and into the vacuum steps the entrepreneur with the clearest, most human-shaped villain on offer. Sideways is simply the path of least resistance when no one is making the case for up.

I want to be careful here, because the comparison flatters no one. France has its own sideways channel, and it is powerful — it just wears a different rosette. The gilets jaunes had their ugly moments too. The difference isn't moral. It's structural. It's about who happens to be holding the microphone when people are frightened, and what they choose to say into it.

I work on digital identity, so let me be precise about one thing: the tool is not the problem. A verifiable identity is neutral. What matters is the story you decide to tell around it — and a democracy ought to be able to address precarity without needing an internal enemy to make the problem legible.

The frightening part isn't that people are angry. People have every reason to be. The frightening part is how cheaply that anger can be redirected, and how few voices are bothering to point it upward.
